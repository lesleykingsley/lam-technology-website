Broker, Platform or Advisor: Who Should Source Your Network Project?

Published October 2026

Four kinds of companies will offer to help you source a network project. They are paid differently and they work differently. Here is how to tell which one fits, and what to ask before you sign anything.

By LAM Technology

The short answer. If you know exactly what you need and only want quotes, a platform or broker can be enough. If you are not sure what to buy, the project spans several suppliers, or your team is lean, an independent advisor adds value before the order and after it. The deciding factor is rarely the price quote. It is what the firm does besides place the order, and how it is paid.

How LAM is paid, plainly

Suppliers sell through two routes: their own direct sales force and a distribution channel. LAM is part of that channel. When you choose a supplier through LAM for sourcing work, the supplier compensates LAM, in the same way it compensates its own sales team.

What that does and does not mean:

LAM does not resell. The supplier you choose bills you directly. Your pricing matches what that supplier would offer you buying direct.

Professional services are paid by the client. Billing Breakthrough™ is one example. LAM charges only for its own professional services.

LAM does not publish what suppliers pay it. That is a fair thing to weigh. Any time a supplier pays the firm advising you, you cannot see the incentive from outside. Two checks protect you, and they work with any firm: ask a supplier for a direct quote and compare it, and ask whether the firm will show you every quote it received, not only the one it recommends.

Questions to ask any of them

Who pays you, and could that change what you recommend? Expect a direct answer. Be cautious if you get a vague one.

Can I get a direct quote to compare? A firm confident in its pricing will not object.

Which suppliers can you quote for my sites? A limited list means limited options at some addresses.

Do you sell or resell any service yourself? That is a different incentive than advising.

What do you do after the order is placed? Installations slip and bills arrive wrong. Find out who you call.

Have these ready: the address of every site, your current circuits with contract end dates, the bandwidth and uptime each site needs, your target timeline, and who has authority to sign. A firm that asks for these first, before quoting anything, is usually the one worth your time.

Common questions

Is a telecom broker free to the buyer?

Usually the buyer does not pay a broker directly. The carrier you choose compensates the broker. Many advisors, including LAM on sourcing work, are compensated by suppliers in a similar way. So the useful questions are what the firm does besides place the order, and whether your price matches what you would be offered buying direct.

What is the difference between a broker and an advisor?

There is no formal definition, and both can be paid by suppliers. The practical difference is scope. A broker's work often ends when the order is placed. An advisor assesses the need first, compares suppliers, and stays involved after installation. Ask any firm to put its scope in writing.

What should I have ready before I ask for circuit quotes?

A list of site addresses, your current circuits with contract end dates, the bandwidth and uptime each site needs, your target timeline, and who has authority to sign.
